PAS 7 Statement of Cash Flows

PAS 7 tells you how to present where an entity's cash came from and where it went during the period. Every cash flow is sorted into operating, investing or financing activities. Only transactions that actually moved cash or cash equivalents go in the statement; the rest are disclosed. Operating cash flows can be shown with the direct or the indirect method, and the exam mostly tests classification, the 3-month rule for cash equivalents, and squeezing cash amounts out of accrual figures.

Overview and objective

The statement of cash flows shows the historical changes in cash and cash equivalents (sources and uses) during the period. It is part of a complete set of financial statements.

Used with the other statements, it helps users judge:

  • the entity's ability to generate cash and cash equivalents
  • the timing and certainty of those cash flows
  • the entity's needs for cash

It also says something about quality of earnings: an entity can report a profit under accrual accounting yet have negative operating cash flows (for example, when receivables are hard to collect). Because it is prepared on a cash basis, it improves comparability between entities that use different accounting policies for the same transactions.

Key definitions: cash and cash equivalents

  • Cash: cash on hand and demand deposits (cash in bank).
  • Cash equivalents: short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible to known amounts of cash and have an insignificant risk of changes in value.
  • Cash flows: inflows and outflows of cash and cash equivalents.

The 3-month rule: a debt instrument is a cash equivalent only if it was acquired within 3 months of its maturity date. It is the term remaining when acquired that counts, not the time left at year-end.

General rule: what goes in the statement

Only transactions that affected cash and cash equivalents are reported. Non-cash transactions are excluded and disclosed only.

Include, for example, the purchase of an asset paid in cash.
Exclude, for example:

  • buying an asset by issuing a note payable or shares
  • converting debt into equity
  • stock dividends

If part of a transaction is paid in cash (such as a 10% down payment on equipment with a note for the rest), only the cash part is reported: the down payment is an investing outflow and the note is disclosed as non-cash.

Special operating items:

  • Cash flows from held-for-trading securities are operating; they are like inventory, bought to resell.
  • An entity that routinely makes or buys PPE to rent out and later sell treats the acquisition, rentals and sale proceeds as operating.
  • Loans of a bank or other financial institution are operating, because lending is its main revenue activity.

Trade versus non-trade liabilities: payments of trade payables, accrued expenses and other operating liabilities are operating. Only non-trade (non-operating) liabilities produce financing cash flows.

  • Option 1 reasoning: interest and dividend income and interest expense enter profit or loss; dividends paid are transactions with owners.
  • Option 2 reasoning: income from investments is investing; interest expense is a cost of borrowing; dividends paid are shown as operating to help users judge whether the entity can pay dividends out of operations.

Rules to remember:

  • Financial institutions may use Option 1 only.
  • If an exam problem is silent, assume Option 1.
  • Only interest and dividends actually received or paid are reported. Dividends declared in Year 1 and paid in Year 2 appear only in Year 2.

Items left out of the three activities

  • Moving money between cash and cash equivalents (such as buying a 60-day placement) is cash management, not an activity.
  • Bank overdrafts: if they cannot be offset against cash, they are financing activities. If they are repayable on demand and part of cash management, they are part of cash and cash equivalents.
  • Foreign currency: cash flows are translated at the spot rate on the date of the flow. Exchange differences are not cash flows, but the effect of rate changes on cash held in a foreign currency is shown separately, to reconcile beginning and ending cash.

Presentation: direct and indirect method

For operating activities only, PAS 7 allows two methods:

  • Direct method: shows each major class of gross cash receipts and gross cash payments. PAS 7 encourages it because it helps estimate future cash flows.
  • Indirect method: starts from profit or loss and adjusts for non-cash items and changes in operating assets and liabilities. It is the method used more in practice.

Both give the same net cash from operating activities. Investing and financing activities always show gross receipts and gross payments separately, unless they qualify for net presentation.

How to compute the direct-method lines (the squeezes)

Each line starts from an income-statement amount and is adjusted by its related balance-sheet accounts. When in doubt, draw the T-account and squeeze the cash figure.

How to build the indirect method

  1. Start with profit (the book's illustration starts with profit before tax).
  2. Add back non-cash expenses and losses: depreciation, amortization, impairment, losses on sale of assets, discount amortization on bonds payable.
  3. Deduct non-cash income and gains: gains on sale, unrealized gains on trading securities, share in profit of an associate.
  4. In the book's format, also add back interest expense and deduct interest and dividend income, because the actual cash interest and dividends are shown as separate lines.
  5. Adjust for working capital: increase in an operating asset → deduct; decrease → add. Increase in an operating liability → add; decrease → deduct.
  6. The subtotal is cash generated from operations. Then show interest paid, interest and dividends received (Option 1) and income taxes paid.

Example (from the book's illustration): profit before tax 250, depreciation +30, gain on sale (10), impairment +5, interest expense +12, increase in receivables (150), decrease in inventories +3, decrease in payables (60) → cash generated 80; interest paid (10); taxes paid (20) → net cash from operating activities 50. The direct method gives the same 50.

Subsidiaries and other business units

  • Acquiring or disposing of a subsidiary when control is obtained or lost → investing activity, shown net of the cash acquired or disposed of.
  • Changes in ownership that do not change control (buying more shares of a subsidiary you already control) → financing activity, as a transaction with owners.

Disclosure

  • The components of cash and cash equivalents, reconciled to the amounts in the statement of financial position.
  • Significant cash balances held by the entity that are not available for use by the group, with management's commentary.
  • Non-cash investing and financing transactions.

Common exam traps

  • Using the time to maturity at year-end instead of when acquired for the 3-month rule.
  • Reporting dividends declared instead of dividends paid.
  • Treating trade payables as financing (they are operating).
  • Putting held-for-trading purchases in investing (they are operating).
  • Adding back bad debt expense while using the change in net receivables.
  • Counting a whole asset purchase as cash when part was paid with a note or shares.
  • Thinking the direct/indirect choice applies to investing and financing too.
  • Assuming Option 2 when the problem is silent: the default is Option 1.

PAS 8 Accounting Policies, Changes in Accounting Estimates and Errors

PAS 8 prescribes the criteria for selecting and changing accounting policies, together with the accounting treatment and disclosure of changes in accounting policies, changes in accounting estimates, and corrections of prior period errors. It establishes the hierarchy of reporting standards to guide management in the absence of a specific PFRS. Understanding the distinction between retrospective application for policy changes/errors and prospective application for estimate changes is critical for departmental and board examinations.

Overview and objective

PAS 8 establishes the criteria for selecting, applying, and changing accounting policies, as well as the accounting treatment for changes in accounting estimates and prior period errors.

  • Objective: To enhance the relevance, reliability, and comparability of an entity's financial statements over time and across different entities.
  • Consistency Rule: An entity must select and apply its accounting policies consistently for similar transactions, other events, and conditions.

Scope (what it covers and what it excludes)

PAS 8 applies strictly to:

  • Selection and application of accounting policies
  • Accounting for changes in accounting policies
  • Accounting for changes in accounting estimates
  • Accounting for corrections of prior period errors

Exclusions / Special Rules:

  • Initial adoption of a policy to carry assets at revalued amounts under PAS 16 or PAS 38 is treated as a revaluation rather than under PAS 8 retrospective rules.
  • Tax effects of adjustments arising from changes in accounting policies or errors are accounted for under PAS 12 (Income Taxes).

Key definitions

  • Accounting policies: Specific principles, bases, conventions, rules, and practices applied by an entity in preparing and presenting financial statements.
  • Change in accounting estimate: An adjustment of the carrying amount of an asset/liability or periodic consumption of an asset resulting from new information or developments.
  • Prior period errors: Omissions from and misstatements in financial statements for one or more prior periods arising from failure to use or misuse of reliable information.
  • Retrospective application: Applying a new accounting policy to transactions as if that policy had always been applied.
  • Retrospective restatement: Correcting the recognition, measurement, and disclosure of amounts as if a prior period error had never occurred.
  • Prospective application: Applying a new policy or recognizing the effect of an estimate change in current and future periods affected.

Hierarchy of guidance in selecting accounting policies

In the absence of a PFRS that specifically applies to a transaction, management must use judgment to develop a policy that results in relevant and reliable information.

Mandatory Order of Hierarchy (Descending Authority):

  1. Primary Source: Requirements in PFRSs dealing with similar and related issues.
  2. Secondary Source: Definitions, recognition criteria, and measurement concepts in the Conceptual Framework.
  3. Other Allowed Sources: Most recent pronouncements of other standard-setting bodies using a similar conceptual framework, other accounting literature, and accepted industry practices.

Accounting treatment: Changes in accounting policies

General Rule: Accounting policies must be applied consistently.

When Permitted:

  • Required by a PFRS/PAS standard or interpretation.
  • Voluntary change results in financial statements providing more relevant and reliably represented information.

Application Method:

  • Follow specific transitional provisions if required by a new standard.
  • If no transitional provisions exist or if voluntary, apply RETROSPECTIVELY.
  • Adjustment is recognized against the opening balance of Retained Earnings of the earliest period presented.
  • Comparative prior period statements presented must be restated.

Accounting treatment: Changes in accounting estimates

General Rule: Revisions of estimates arise from new information, more experience, or subsequent developments. They are normal recurring adjustments.

Application Method:

  • Accounted for PROSPECTIVELY by including it in profit or loss in:
  1. The period of change (if affecting that period only)
  2. The period of change and future periods (if affecting both)
  • Retrospective restatement is strictly prohibited.

Inseparable Rule: When it is difficult to distinguish between a change in policy and a change in estimate, the change is treated entirely as a change in accounting estimate (prospective).

Accounting treatment: Prior period errors

General Rule: Prior period errors result from mathematical mistakes, mistakes in applying policies, misinterpretation of facts, fraud, or oversight.

Application Method:

  • Corrected RETROSPECTIVELY.
  • Restate the comparative amounts for the prior period(s) presented in which the error occurred.
  • If the error occurred before the earliest prior period presented, adjust the opening balance of Retained Earnings for the earliest prior period presented.

Journal entries or illustrations with numbers

Illustration 1: Change in Accounting Estimate (Depreciation)
An equipment costing P500,000 with an initial 5-year life (no residual value) is depreciated for 2 years (P100,000/yr). Accumulated Depreciation = P200,000; Carrying Amount = P300,000. At the start of Year 3, remaining life is revised to 6 years.

  • Revised annual depreciation = P300,000 / 6 years = P50,000 per year.

Journal Entry (Year 3 onwards):

  • Debit: Depreciation Expense P50,000
  • Credit: Accumulated Depreciation P50,000

Entity changes from FIFO to Weighted Average at the start of 2021. Dec 31, 2020 FIFO Inventory = P1,000,000; Weighted Average Inventory = P750,000. Decrease in beginning inventory = P250,000.

Journal Entry (Jan 1, 2021):

  • Debit: Retained Earnings (Beginning) P250,000

How to compute (formulas, order of steps, and schedules to use)

  1. Steps for Change in Depreciation Estimate:
  • Step 1: Compute Carrying Amount at date of change:

$$\text{Carrying Amount} = \text{Historical Cost} - \text{Accumulated Depreciation to date}$$

  • Step 2: Compute Depreciable Base:

$$\text{New Base} = \text{Carrying Amount} - \text{Revised Residual Value}$$

  • Step 3: Compute New Annual Depreciation:

$$\text{New Depreciation} = \frac{\text{New Base}}{\text{Revised Remaining Useful Life}}$$

  1. Steps for Retrospective Restatement / Policy Change:
  • Step 1: Compute cumulative net income impact for all prior periods up to the start of the current year.
  • Step 2: Record journal entry adjusting opening Retained Earnings and related balance sheet accounts.
  • Step 3: Restate prior period comparative figures presented in the statements.

Common exam traps

  • Trap 1: Depreciation Method Change: Changing depreciation method (e.g., from Straight-Line to SYD or Double Declining) is a change in accounting estimate (prospective), NOT a change in policy!
  • Trap 2: Inseparable Changes: If a change in policy cannot be distinguished from an estimate change, treat it as an estimate change (prospective).
  • Trap 3: Cash Basis to Accrual Basis: Changing from Cash Basis to Accrual Basis is correction of an error (since cash basis is non-GAAP), NOT a change in policy.
  • Trap 4: Revaluation Model First Adoption: Initial adoption of revaluation model for PPE is treated under PAS 16 as a revaluation, NOT retrospective under PAS 8.

PAS 24 Related Party Disclosures

PAS 24 deals with identifying related party relationships and disclosing related party transactions, outstanding balances, commitments, and certain relationships even when no transaction occurred. The main exam skill is determining whether two persons or entities are related based on control, joint control, significant influence, key management status, or close family relationships. A major trap is assuming that parties are related merely because they transact heavily with each other, share one director, or deal with the same government or utility. Once a related party relationship or transaction exists, the focus shifts to what information must be disclosed in the financial statements.

Overview and objective

PAS 24 prescribes guidelines for identifying related party relationships, related party transactions, outstanding balances and commitments, and the disclosures required for them.

Related party relationships are common in business. For example, a parent and its subsidiaries are related parties. The importance of PAS 24 is that relationships between parties may affect the financial position and performance of an entity because related parties may transact on terms that unrelated parties would not accept.

The relationship itself may also influence decisions even if no transaction occurs. For example, a parent may influence a subsidiary's choice of supplier even without directly entering into a transaction with the subsidiary.

Therefore, users need information about related party relationships, transactions, balances and commitments so that they can better assess the risks and opportunities surrounding the entity.

Scope

The discussion in Millan focuses on:

  • identifying related parties;
  • identifying persons or entities that are not related parties;
  • relationships between parents and subsidiaries;
  • key management personnel compensation;
  • related party transactions and outstanding balances;
  • disclosures in separate or individual financial statements and their elimination in consolidated financial statements; and
  • government-related entities.

PAS 24 is primarily a disclosure standard in this chapter. Millan does not prescribe a separate recognition, measurement, depreciation, valuation or journal-entry method merely because a transaction is with a related party.

Key definitions

Related parties are parties whose relationship gives one party the ability to affect the financial and operating decisions of another through control, significant influence or joint control.

Key management personnel are persons having authority and responsibility for planning, directing and controlling the activities of the entity, directly or indirectly, including any director, whether executive or otherwise.

Close family member is a person who may be expected to influence, or be influenced by, another person in that person's dealings with the reporting entity. Millan includes the person's spouse, children and dependents.

Related party transaction is a transfer of resources, services or obligations between a reporting entity and a related party, regardless of whether a price is charged.

Government-related entity is an entity that is controlled, jointly controlled or significantly influenced by a government.

Presentation and disclosure

Relationships between parents and subsidiaries

A parent-subsidiary relationship is disclosed even if there were no transactions between the parties during the period.

A subsidiary discloses:

  • the name of its parent;
  • if different, the name of its ultimate parent; and
  • if neither the parent nor ultimate parent prepares consolidated financial statements for public use, the name of the next most senior parent that does so.

Key management personnel compensation

An entity discloses total key management personnel compensation and breaks it down into the required categories.

Related party transactions

Examples of transactions disclosed when they involve a related party include:

  • purchases or sales of goods, services or other assets;
  • leases;
  • transfers of research and development;
  • transfers under license agreements;
  • loans and other financing arrangements, including equity contributions;
  • provision of guarantees or collateral;
  • commitments;
  • settlement of liabilities by or on behalf of either party; and
  • participation by a parent or subsidiary in a defined benefit plan in which risks are shared.

When related party transactions occurred during the periods covered by the financial statements, disclose:

  • the nature of the related party relationship;
  • the nature, terms and amount of the transaction and outstanding balances; and
  • doubtful debts recognized on the outstanding balances.

Special cases and exceptions

Examples of related parties

Millan gives the following examples:

  1. Parent and its subsidiary.
  2. Fellow subsidiaries with a common parent.
  3. Investor and its associate, and the associate's subsidiary.
  4. Venturer and the joint venture, and the joint venture's subsidiary.
  5. A joint venture and an associate of a common investor.
  6. Key management personnel of the reporting entity or of the reporting entity's parent.
  7. A person who has control, significant influence or joint control over the reporting entity.
  8. A close family member of the persons described in items 6 and 7.
  9. A post-employment benefit plan for employees of the reporting entity or an entity related to the reporting entity.

Parties that are not automatically related

A relationship is not established merely because two parties interact, share certain personnel, or are economically dependent on one another.

The following are not related parties merely because of the stated circumstance:

  • two entities simply because they have one director or key management personnel in common;
  • two joint venturers simply because they are co-venturers in a joint venture;
  • financiers, trade unions, public utilities and government agencies that do not control, jointly control or significantly influence the reporting entity, merely because of normal dealings with the entity; and
  • a customer, supplier or other business with which the entity has significant transactions merely because of economic dependence.

Government-related entities

A government-related entity is an entity controlled, jointly controlled or significantly influenced by a government.

If a government-related entity has related party transactions with the government, Millan requires disclosure of:

  • the name of the government and the nature of the relationship;
  • the nature and amount of each individually significant transaction; and
  • other transactions that are collectively significant but individually insignificant.

Separate versus consolidated financial statements

Related party transactions and outstanding balances are disclosed in an entity's separate or individual financial statements but are eliminated in the group's consolidated financial statements when they are intragroup transactions and balances.

This is a common conceptual trap. A transaction between a parent and subsidiary may need disclosure in their separate financial statements because they are related parties. However, when preparing consolidated financial statements, transactions and balances within the group are eliminated because the group is presented as a single economic entity.

Arm's length disclosures

An entity should not state that related party transactions were made on an arm's length basis unless that statement can be substantiated.

'Arm's length' means the transaction is claimed to have been conducted on terms comparable to those that would exist between independent parties. PAS 24 does not allow an entity to make this claim merely because management believes the terms were fair.

Common exam traps

  1. Do not assume that parties are related merely because they transact with each other. A major customer or supplier is not automatically a related party simply because of economic dependence.
  2. A common director does not automatically make two entities related. The director may be related to each entity, while the entities themselves are not related merely because of the interlocking director.
  3. Parent-subsidiary relationships are disclosed even without transactions. Do not look only for actual sales, loans or balances.
  4. A related party transaction can exist even when no price is charged. The definition includes transfers of resources, services or obligations regardless of whether consideration is paid.
  5. Separate financial statements and consolidated financial statements are different. Related party transactions and balances may be disclosed in the separate statements but eliminated on consolidation.
  6. Do not automatically call normal dealings with banks, utilities, unions or government agencies related party transactions. Control, joint control or significant influence must be considered.
  7. Do not claim that transactions were at arm's length unless this can be substantiated.
  8. When a person is key management personnel, analyze the relationship of that person to each entity separately before deciding whether the entities themselves are related.

Give the classic AICPA definition of accounting.

The art of recording, classifying and summarizing, in a significant manner and in terms of money, transactions and events that are at least partly financial in character, and interpreting the results.

What is the basic purpose of accounting?

To provide quantitative information, primarily financial, about economic entities that is useful in making economic decisions.

Name the three basic activities of accounting.

Identifying (which events are accountable), measuring (assigning peso amounts), and communicating (recording, classifying, summarizing and interpreting through reports).

Bookkeeping vs accounting: what’s the difference?

Bookkeeping is the recording phase: mechanical and procedural. Accounting is broader: it includes bookkeeping plus system design, analysis and interpretation.

Which law regulates accountancy in the Philippines?

RA 9298, the Philippine Accountancy Act of 2004. It covers the practice of accountancy, the CPA licensure exam, registration, and the Professional Regulatory Board of Accountancy (BOA).

What are the four sectors of accountancy practice under RA 9298?

Public practice, commerce and industry, education/academe, and government.

How is the Board of Accountancy composed?

A chairman and six members appointed by the President of the Philippines, under the supervision of the Professional Regulation Commission. Each serves a three-year term.

What do you need to pass the CPA Licensure Exam?

A general weighted average of at least 75%, with no rating below 65% in any subject.

Who sets financial reporting standards in the Philippines?

The Financial Reporting Standards Council (FRSC), which replaced the Accounting Standards Council. It adopts IFRS as PFRS. The Philippine Interpretations Committee (PIC) issues interpretations.

When no PFRS specifically applies, what hierarchy does management follow?
  1. Requirements in PFRS dealing with similar and related issues; 2) the Conceptual Framework’s definitions, recognition criteria and measurement concepts. It may also consider pronouncements of other standard-setters with a similar framework, industry practice and literature, if they don’t conflict.
Name the common branches of accounting.

Financial accounting, management accounting, cost accounting, auditing, taxation, government accounting, accounting education, and accounting research.

Which reporting framework applies by entity size under SEC rules?

Large or publicly accountable (assets over ₱350M or liabilities over ₱250M): full PFRS. Medium (assets ₱100M–₱350M or liabilities ₱100M–₱250M): PFRS for SMEs. Small (₱3M–₱100M): PFRS for Small Entities. Micro (under ₱3M): income tax basis or another acceptable basis.

Is the Conceptual Framework a PFRS?

No. It is not a standard, and nothing in it overrides any PFRS or any requirement in a PFRS.

What are the three purposes of the Conceptual Framework?

Help the Board develop standards based on consistent concepts; help preparers develop consistent policies when no standard applies or a choice is allowed; help all parties understand and interpret the standards.

What is the objective of general purpose financial reporting?

To provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions about providing resources to the entity.

Who are the primary users of general purpose financial reports?

Existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors.

What are the fundamental qualitative characteristics and their components?

Relevance (predictive value, confirmatory value, materiality) and faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from error).

Name the enhancing qualitative characteristics.

Comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability.

When is information material?

When omitting, misstating or obscuring it could reasonably be expected to influence decisions primary users make based on the financial reports. Materiality is an entity-specific aspect of relevance.

What is prudence under the 2018 Framework?

The exercise of caution when making judgments under uncertainty. It supports neutrality and does not allow overstating or understating assets, liabilities, income or expenses.

What is the underlying assumption of financial statements?

Going concern: the entity is assumed to continue operating for the foreseeable future, with no intention or need to liquidate or curtail operations.

Define an asset (2018 Framework).

A present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. An economic resource is a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits.

Define a liability (2018 Framework).

A present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events.

Define income and expenses.

Income: increases in assets or decreases in liabilities that increase equity, other than contributions from holders of equity claims. Expenses: decreases in assets or increases in liabilities that decrease equity, other than distributions to holders of equity claims.

What are the recognition criteria under the 2018 Framework?

An item that meets the definition of an element is recognized if recognition provides relevant information and a faithful representation of it.

What measurement bases does the Framework describe?

Historical cost and current value. Current value includes fair value, value in use (assets) or fulfilment value (liabilities), and current cost.

Financial vs physical capital maintenance?

Financial capital: profit is earned if net assets at the end exceed the beginning, in nominal pesos or constant purchasing power. Physical capital: profit is earned if productive capacity at the end exceeds the beginning; price changes are capital maintenance adjustments in equity, not profit.

What is the cost constraint?

Reporting financial information imposes costs, and those costs should be justified by the benefits of reporting it.

What does PFRS 18 replace, and when is it effective?

It replaces PAS 1. It applies to annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, with earlier application permitted, and is applied retrospectively.

What are the five categories in the statement of profit or loss under PFRS 18?

Operating, investing, financing, income taxes, and discontinued operations.

What new subtotals does PFRS 18 require?

Operating profit or loss, and profit or loss before financing and income taxes, in addition to profit or loss.

Which category is the default under PFRS 18?

Operating. It is the residual category: income and expenses not classified in investing, financing, income taxes or discontinued operations.

What goes in the investing category?

Income and expenses from equity-method investments in associates and joint ventures, cash and cash equivalents, and other assets that generate a return individually and largely independently of the entity’s other resources (for an entity without a specified main business activity).

What goes in the financing category?

Income and expenses from liabilities that only raise finance (such as interest on loans), plus interest expense and effects of interest-rate changes on other liabilities.

What are management-defined performance measures (MPMs)?

Subtotals of income and expenses used in public communications outside the financial statements to show management’s view of performance. They are disclosed in a single note, reconciled to the most comparable PFRS subtotal, with the tax and NCI effects.

How may operating expenses be presented, and what extra note does PFRS 18 require?

By nature, by function, or a mix. If any are presented by function, disclose in one note the amounts of depreciation, amortization, employee benefits, impairment losses and inventory write-downs.

Into which two groups is OCI split? Give examples.

Items that will not be reclassified to profit or loss (revaluation surplus, remeasurements of defined benefit plans, equity investments at FVOCI) and items that will be reclassified when conditions are met (debt investments at FVOCI, translation of a foreign operation, effective portion of cash flow hedges).

What is a complete set of financial statements?

Statement of financial position, statement(s) of financial performance (profit or loss and comprehensive income), statement of changes in equity, statement of cash flows, notes, and comparative information.

When is a third statement of financial position required?

When a policy is applied retrospectively, items are restated, or items are reclassified, and the change has a material effect on the statement at the beginning of the preceding period.

What do PFRS 18’s aggregation and disaggregation principles say?

Aggregate items with shared characteristics and disaggregate items with dissimilar ones. Use the label “other” only when no more informative label is available.

When is an asset classified as current?

When it is expected to be realized, sold or consumed in the normal operating cycle; held primarily for trading; expected to be realized within 12 months after the reporting period; or is cash or a cash equivalent not restricted for at least 12 months.

When is a liability classified as current?

When it is expected to be settled in the normal operating cycle; held primarily for trading; due within 12 months after the reporting period; or the entity has no right at the end of the reporting period to defer settlement for at least 12 months.

A loan due within 12 months is refinanced after the reporting date. Current or noncurrent?

Current. Refinancing after the reporting period doesn’t change classification. It is noncurrent only if, at the end of the reporting period, the entity had the right to roll it over for at least 12 months under an existing facility.

A covenant breach makes a long-term loan payable on demand. Classification?

Current, unless by the end of the reporting period the lender agreed to a grace period ending at least 12 months after the reporting date, during which it can’t demand repayment.

Do covenants the entity must meet after the reporting date affect classification?

No. Only covenants the entity must comply with on or before the reporting date affect the right to defer settlement. Future covenants are disclosed instead.

What is the operating cycle?

The time between acquiring assets for processing and their realization in cash. When it isn’t clearly identifiable, it is assumed to be 12 months.

How are deferred tax assets and liabilities classified?

Never as current, when the entity uses a current/noncurrent classification.

What line item did PFRS 18 add to the statement of financial position?

Goodwill, as a separate required line item.

When is a liquidity-based presentation allowed instead of current/noncurrent?

When it provides information that is reliable and more relevant, such as for financial institutions. Assets and liabilities are then presented in order of liquidity.

May assets and liabilities be offset?

No, unless a PFRS requires or permits it.

What are cash equivalents?

Short-term, highly liquid investments readily convertible to known amounts of cash and subject to insignificant risk of changes in value, normally maturing within three months from the date of acquisition.

Are bank overdrafts part of cash and cash equivalents?

Yes, when they are repayable on demand and form an integral part of the entity’s cash management.

Name the three classes of cash flows and give an example of each.

Operating (cash from customers, payments to suppliers and employees), investing (buying or selling PPE and investments), financing (issuing shares, borrowing, repaying loans).

Under the PFRS 18 amendments to PAS 7, where do interest and dividends go?

For entities without a specified main business activity: interest paid and dividends paid in financing; interest received and dividends received in investing. The old free choice is removed.

How are income taxes paid classified?

Operating, unless they can be specifically identified with investing or financing activities.

What is the starting point of the indirect method after PFRS 18?

Operating profit or loss (previously profit or loss).

Indirect method: how do changes in working capital adjust profit?

Add decreases in current operating assets and increases in current operating liabilities. Deduct increases in current operating assets and decreases in current operating liabilities. Add back noncash expenses like depreciation; deduct gains on sale of noncurrent assets.

How are noncash investing and financing transactions reported?

They are excluded from the statement of cash flows and disclosed elsewhere, for example acquiring an asset by issuing shares, or converting debt to equity.

How do you compute cash collected from customers (direct method)?

Sales minus the increase in accounts receivable (or plus the decrease), adjusted for write-offs where relevant.

Which method does PAS 7 encourage for operating activities?

The direct method, because it shows information useful for estimating future cash flows. The indirect method is still allowed.

How are changes in policy, changes in estimate, and prior-period errors applied?

Change in policy: retrospectively (unless impracticable). Change in estimate: prospectively. Prior-period error: retrospective restatement.

Give examples of changes in accounting estimates.

Useful life, residual value or depreciation method of PPE; allowance for doubtful accounts; inventory obsolescence; warranty obligations; fair value of financial assets.

It’s hard to tell a change in policy from a change in estimate. What now?

Treat it as a change in accounting estimate.

What is NOT a change in accounting policy?

Applying a policy to transactions that differ in substance from those before, and applying a new policy to transactions that didn’t occur before or were immaterial.

How is a material prior-period error corrected?

Retrospectively, by restating comparatives for the prior periods presented, or if the error happened before the earliest period presented, restating the opening balances of assets, liabilities and equity (usually retained earnings).

What did PFRS 18 rename PAS 8?

“Basis of Preparation of Financial Statements”. Some general requirements moved there from PAS 1, such as going concern and fair presentation.

PAS 10: adjusting vs non-adjusting events?

Adjusting events give evidence of conditions that existed at the end of the reporting period (settling a court case, customer bankruptcy, discovering fraud or errors). Non-adjusting events reflect conditions that arose after (a fire destroying a plant, a decline in market value of investments, a major business combination).

Dividends declared after the reporting period: liability?

No. No obligation existed at the end of the reporting period. They are disclosed in the notes.

Going concern deteriorates after the reporting period. What happens?

The financial statements are not prepared on a going concern basis if management decides to liquidate or stop trading, or has no realistic alternative.

Which date must the entity disclose under PAS 10?

The date the financial statements were authorized for issue, and who gave that authorization.

When is a person (or close family member) a related party of an entity?

When the person has control, joint control or significant influence over the entity, or is a member of the key management personnel of the entity or its parent.

Who are NOT automatically related parties under PAS 24?

Two entities simply because they share a director; providers of finance, trade unions, utilities and government agencies in normal dealings; and a single customer or supplier with significant business volume.

What key management personnel compensation must be disclosed?

In total and by category: short-term employee benefits, post-employment benefits, other long-term benefits, termination benefits, and share-based payment.

Must a parent–subsidiary relationship be disclosed with no transactions?

Yes. Relationships between a parent and its subsidiaries are disclosed whether or not there were transactions.

When is a provision recognized?

When there is a present obligation (legal or constructive) from a past event, an outflow of resources is probable, and the amount can be reliably estimated.

Provision vs contingent liability vs contingent asset: how are they reported?

Probable outflow: recognize a provision. Possible outflow: disclose a contingent liability. Remote: no disclosure. Contingent asset: never recognized; disclosed when inflow is probable; recognized only when virtually certain (then it isn’t contingent).

How is a provision measured?

At the best estimate of the expenditure required to settle it. Large population: expected value. Single obligation: most likely outcome. Equally likely continuous range: the midpoint. Discount when the time value of money is material.

Which costs belong in a restructuring provision?

Only direct expenditures that are necessarily entailed and not associated with ongoing activities. Exclude retraining or relocating continuing staff, marketing, and investment in new systems and distribution networks.

What is an onerous contract and how is it measured?

A contract whose unavoidable costs exceed the expected benefits. The provision is the lower of the cost of fulfilling it and the penalty for exiting it.

Can a provision be recognized for future operating losses?

No. Future operating losses don’t meet the definition of a liability.

How are reimbursements of a provision treated (e.g., insurance)?

Recognized only when virtually certain, as a separate asset not exceeding the provision. In profit or loss, the expense may be shown net of the reimbursement.

Chapter 13: Financial Futures Markets

Financial futures markets provide standardized, exchange-traded derivative contracts that obligate market participants to buy or sell financial instruments at a predetermined price on a future delivery date. This topic focuses heavily on the mechanics of futures trading, including margin accounts, daily mark-to-market adjustments, and position offset. For examinations, the most tested areas are distinguishing between speculative and hedging strategies (long versus short), calculating financial leverage returns and nominal profits, interpreting IMM index quotations for Treasury bills, executing stock index arbitrage using the cost of carry model, and identifying the six major risks of futures trading.

Overview

A financial futures contract is a legally binding, standardized agreement traded on an organized exchange (such as the CME Group) to buy or sell a specified quantity of a financial instrument at a specified price on a designated future settlement date.

  • Exchange Standardization: Contracts have uniform specifications including contract size, delivery months, and minimum tick sizes.
  • Clearinghouse Guarantee: The exchange clearinghouse acts as the counterparty to every transaction (buyer to every seller, seller to every buyer), eliminating primary counterparty default risk.
  • Dual Roles: Market participants are categorized as either Hedgers (institutions seeking to reduce preexisting balance sheet or portfolio risks) or Speculators (traders seeking profit by taking unhedged positions based on interest rate or price forecasts).
  • Settlement Method: Over 99% of financial futures contracts are closed out prior to maturity via an offsetting trade rather than through physical asset delivery.

Key concepts

The fundamental principles governing financial futures operations include:

  • Inverse Price-Yield Relationship: Debt securities and interest rate futures prices move in the exact opposite direction of interest rates. When interest rates rise, bond and futures prices fall. When interest rates fall, bond and futures prices rise.
  • Long Position (Buyer): Agrees to purchase the underlying security. A long position profits when the futures price rises (which occurs when interest rates decline).
  • Short Position (Seller): Agrees to deliver/sell the underlying security. A short position profits when the futures price falls (which occurs when interest rates rise).
  • Margin Requirements: Futures traders do not pay the full asset purchase price. Instead, they deposit an Initial Margin (a good-faith deposit of roughly 2% to 10% of face value) to open a trade, and must maintain an equity balance above the Maintenance Margin.
  • Daily Mark-to-Market: At the end of each trading session, the clearinghouse credits daily gains or debits daily losses to each trader's margin account based on the official closing settlement price.
  • Margin Call: If an account balance falls below the maintenance margin level, the broker issues a margin call requiring additional cash (variation margin) to restore the balance back to the initial margin level.
  • Financial Leverage: The use of a small upfront margin deposit to control a large contract value, which magnifies the percentage return on equity (ROI) for even minor shifts in asset market prices.
  • Cross-Hedging: Hedging a spot financial asset with a futures contract on a different but related asset (e.g., hedging corporate bonds using Treasury bond futures). This exposes the hedger to basis risk.
  • Basis and Basis Risk: Basis is defined as the difference between the spot price of the asset being hedged and the futures price of the contract used. Basis risk is the risk that this spread changes unexpectedly over the holding period.

Formulas and when to use each

The core quantitative formulas tested in Chapter 13 include:

  1. IMM Index Quotation for Treasury Bills:

Used to convert between quoted IMM index prices and annualized discount yields on 90-day T-bills.

  • Formula: IMM Index Price = 100 - Annualized Discount Yield
  • Reverse: Annualized Discount Yield = 100 - IMM Index Price
  1. T-Bill Contract Cash Value and Nominal Speculative Profit:

Used to calculate the dollar price and net cash gain or loss on 90-day T-bill futures ($1,000,000 par value).

  • Formula: Contract Price = Par Value * (IMM Index Quote / 100)
  • Formula: Nominal Profit = [(Selling Quote - Buying Quote) / 100] * Par Value
  1. Return on Investment (ROI) via Financial Leverage:

Used to determine the leveraged percentage return on initial margin equity.

  • Formula: ROI on Margin = (Dollar Profit / Initial Margin Deposit) * 100
  • Formula: Leverage Multiplier = Total Contract Face Value / Initial Margin Deposit
  1. Treasury Bond Futures Tick Valuation:

Used for T-bond futures ($100,000 par value) which quote in points and 32nds of a point.

  • Formula: 1 Point = 1% of ￼1,000.00
  • Formula: 1 Tick (1/32 of a point) = ￼31.25
  • Formula: Dollar Change = Number of Ticks * $31.25 * Number of Contracts
  1. Cost of Carry Model (Theoretical Futures Price):

Used to calculate fair value and identify index arbitrage opportunities on stock index futures.

  • Formula: F = S * [1 + (r - q) * t]

Where S = Spot Index, r = Annual risk-free interest borrowing rate, q = Annual dividend yield, and t = Time to maturity in years.

Step-by-step method

Follow these structured procedural steps when solving examination problems:

  1. Evaluating a Speculative Strategy:
  • Step 1: Identify the interest rate forecast. If rates are forecasted to fall, execute a Long Position (Buy). If rates are forecasted to rise, execute a Short Position (Sell).
  • Step 2: Determine contract price changes using either decimal points (for T-bills) or 32nds (for T-bonds).
  • Step 3: Compute gross dollar profit by multiplying the price change by contract par value and the number of contracts.
  • Step 4: Calculate the leveraged ROI by dividing the profit by the initial margin deposit.
  1. Evaluating an Institutional Hedge:
  • Step 1: Determine the spot risk exposure. An existing bond portfolio or fixed-rate loan portfolio loses value when rates rise (requires a Short Hedge). An anticipated future cash inflow faces lower reinvestment yields if rates fall (requires a Long Hedge).
  • Step 2: Compute the expected dollar loss (or increased cost) in the spot market.
  • Step 3: Compute the offsetting dollar gain generated by the futures position.
  • Step 4: Net the spot change against the futures change. If a cross-hedge was used, calculate the net variance caused by the change in basis.
  1. Executing Stock Index Arbitrage:
  • Step 1: Compute theoretical futures fair price F* = S * [1 + (r - q) * t].
  • Step 2: Compare actual futures market price (F_actual) with F*.
  • Step 3: If F_actual > F* (overpriced futures), execute cash-and-carry arbitrage: Buy spot stocks, borrow at rate r, and Sell (Short) futures contracts.
  • Step 4: If F_actual < F* (underpriced futures), execute reverse cash-and-carry arbitrage: Short sell spot stocks, invest proceeds at rate r, and Buy (Long) futures contracts.

Common errors

  • Misreading IMM Quotes as Dollar Amounts: Interpreting an IMM quote of 94.50 as ￼94,500 rather than understanding it represents an implied discount yield of 5.50% and a contract price of ￼1 million par.
  • Decimalizing T-Bond Price Ticks: Treating 98-16/32 as 98.16 instead of 98.50 (98 + 16/32).
  • Misidentifying Margin Call Amounts: Calculating variation margin as only the amount needed to return to the maintenance level, instead of the rule-mandated full restoration to the initial margin level.
  • Reversing Long and Short Hedges: Recommending a long hedge when protecting an existing asset against rising rates; an existing asset requires a Short Hedge to offset capital losses.
  • Ignoring Dividends in Cost of Carry: Computing theoretical index futures prices without subtracting the annualized dividend yield from the borrowing financing rate.
  • Confusing Prepayment Risk with Liquidity Risk: Misclassifying early mortgage refinancing during falling rate environments as liquidity risk rather than prepayment risk.

What is a Financial Futures Contract?

A standardized agreement to buy or sell a specified financial instrument at a predetermined price on a specified future date on an organized exchange.

How do Exchange-Traded Futures differ from Over-The-Counter (OTC) Forward Contracts?

Futures are standardized and backed by an exchange clearinghouse; OTC forwards are customized private contracts between two parties with counterparty credit risk.

What is the primary exchange for financial futures trading in the U.S.?

The CME Group, which operates both floor trading and the Globex automated electronic trading platform.

What is a Long Position in financial futures?

An agreement to BUY the underlying security at a specified futures price, profiting when market prices increase.

What is a Short Position in financial futures?

An agreement to SELL the underlying security at a specified futures price, profiting when market prices decline.

What is Initial Margin in futures trading?

The upfront deposit of cash or collateral required by the exchange to open a futures contract position.

What is Maintenance Margin and what triggers a Margin Call?

Maintenance Margin is the minimum equity balance required in a margin account. Falling below this level triggers a Margin Call to deposit funds back to the initial margin level.

How does the Daily Mark-to-Market settlement process work?

At the end of each trading day, gains are added to and losses are debited from the trader's margin account based on the closing settlement price.

How do most traders close out or settle a futures contract prior to expiration?

By taking an Offsetting Position (e.g., selling an identical contract previously bought) before the settlement date.

What is the Cost of Carry Model for valuation of financial futures?

A model stating that the theoretical futures price equals the spot price plus net financing costs (borrowing cost minus income yields).

What is the inverse relationship between market interest rates and bond/futures prices?

When interest rates rise, bond prices and interest rate futures prices fall; when interest rates fall, bond and futures prices rise.

How does a Bullish Speculator trade interest rate futures?

Buys (Long) interest rate futures expecting interest rates to drop, causing bond and futures prices to increase.

How does a Bearish Speculator trade interest rate futures?

Sells (Short) interest rate futures expecting interest rates to rise, causing bond and futures prices to decline.

Why does financial leverage magnify returns and losses in futures trading?

Because traders only deposit a small margin percentage to control a large contract value, small asset price changes cause huge percentage gains or losses on equity.

When and why should an institution execute a Short Hedge?

When expecting interest rates to rise, to protect an existing bond portfolio. Cash profits from short futures offset market value drops on physical bonds.

When and why should an institution execute a Long Hedge?

When expecting interest rates to drop, to lock in a purchase price for planned future asset investments before market prices rise.

What is Cross-Hedging in futures markets?

Hedging a financial asset using a futures contract on a different but related financial asset when an exact match is unavailable.

What is Basis Risk in futures hedging?

The risk that the price of the spot asset and the price of the futures contract do not move in perfect synchronization during a hedge.

What is Stock Index Futures Arbitrage?

Simultaneously trading stock index futures and underlying spot stocks to exploit temporary mispricing between the two markets.

How do arbitrageurs exploit an OVERPRICED stock index futures contract?

They buy the underlying stocks in the spot market and simultaneously sell (short) the overpriced stock index futures contract.

What are Circuit Breakers in stock index futures trading?

Regulatory trading halts triggered when market indexes drop past specified percentage limits to prevent panic selling and restore orderly markets.

What are Single Stock Futures?

Futures contracts on a specific number of shares of a single stock rather than a broad stock index.

How do Commercial Banks participate in interest rate futures markets?

To hedge against interest rate sensitivity mismatches (duration gaps) between their fixed-rate assets and floating-rate liabilities.

How do Pension Funds and Mutual Funds use futures?

To protect equity/bond portfolio values against market drops and lock in reinvestment yields for expected future cash inflows.

What is Market Risk in financial futures?

The risk of financial loss resulting from adverse price movements in the underlying financial instrument.

What is Liquidity Risk in futures trading?

The risk of being unable to quickly close out or offset a futures position at a fair market price due to low trading volume.

What is Credit Risk in futures trading?

The risk that a counterparty defaults on contractual cash obligations (very low in exchange futures due to clearinghouses).

What is Prepayment Risk in interest rate hedging?

The risk that borrowers pay off mortgages or loans earlier than scheduled when interest rates drop, altering the required hedge ratio.

What is Operational Risk in futures trading?

The risk of financial loss due to inadequate internal controls, trading errors, computer system glitches, or unauthorized trading.

Which regulatory body oversees U.S. financial futures markets?

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), which enforces market integrity and protects against fraud and manipulation.

A commercial bank holding fixed-rate mortgages fears interest rates will decline, causing homeowners to refinance early. What specific risk threatens the bank's hedging program?

Prepayment Risk. When market interest rates drop, borrowers refinance early, returning principal sooner than expected and forcing the bank to reinvest at lower prevailing rates.

A financial institution holding long-term fixed-rate loans funded by short-term floating-rate deposits expects interest rates to rise. Which futures strategy should it execute to hedge its balance sheet?

Short Hedge (Sell Interest Rate Futures). As rates rise, bond and futures prices fall, generating a cash gain on the short futures position that offsets the loss in spot loan asset values.

An institutional investor expects to receive a $10 million cash inflow in 3 months and fears interest rates will decline before the funds can be invested. What strategy locks in current higher yields?

Long Hedge (Buy Interest Rate Futures). If rates drop, bond and futures prices rise, generating a cash gain on the futures position that offsets the lower yield on the subsequent spot bond purchase.

What is the key distinction between a Short Hedger and a Bearish Speculator who both sell interest rate futures contracts?

A Short Hedger owns the underlying spot asset (or has existing spot exposure) and trades futures to reduce risk, whereas a Bearish Speculator owns no spot asset and trades purely for unhedged profit.

What is the functional difference between Initial Margin and Maintenance Margin in a futures trading account?

Initial Margin is the upfront cash deposit required to open a contract position, while Maintenance Margin is the minimum account equity balance required to keep the position open without receiving a margin call.

How do financial futures contracts differ fundamentally from over-the-counter (OTC) forward contracts regarding credit risk and customization?

Futures contracts are standardized, exchange-traded, daily marked-to-market, and backed by a clearinghouse (virtually zero credit risk), whereas forward contracts are customized private agreements with significant counterparty credit risk.

A stock index futures contract trades at a price significantly higher than the spot stock index plus net financing costs. How do index arbitrageurs exploit this mispricing?

Buy the underlying stocks in the spot market and sell the stock index futures contract.

Why does a futures position exhibit high financial leverage compared to buying the underlying spot security directly?

Because a trader deposits only a small margin (typically 2% to 10% of contract value), allowing a small percentage change in the underlying asset price to produce a magnified percentage return or loss on invested equity.

An institution wants to hedge a corporate bond portfolio, but no exact futures contract exists for those specific bonds. It hedges using Treasury bond futures instead. What risk arises from this strategy?

Basis Risk (arising from Cross-Hedging). Because corporate bond prices and Treasury bond prices do not move with perfect correlation, the hedge will not perfectly offset spot portfolio value changes.

A Treasury bill futures contract is quoted at an IMM Index Price of 94.20. What is the corresponding annualized discount yield?

5.80%. The formula for T-bill futures pricing is Index Price = 100 - Annualized Discount Yield (100 - 94.20 = 5.80%).

How are gains and losses handled daily in a futures contract margin account?

Through Daily Mark-to-Market settlement, where the account balance is credited or debited at the end of each trading day based on the daily closing settlement price.

How do over 99% of financial futures traders close out their positions prior to the contract expiration date without taking physical delivery?

By executing an Offsetting Trade—taking an equal and opposite position (e.g., buying a contract to close a short position, or selling a contract to close a long position) in the same futures contract.

What happens when an investor's futures margin account balance falls below the maintenance margin level?

The broker issues a Margin Call, requiring the investor to deposit additional funds (variation margin) to bring the account back up to the Initial Margin requirement.

Under the Cost of Carry Model, how is the theoretical fair price of a financial futures contract calculated from the spot price?

Futures Price = Spot Price + Net Financing Cost (where Net Financing Cost equals borrowing interest expense minus asset income yield/dividends).

What is the primary purpose of exchange-imposed Circuit Breakers in financial futures markets?

To temporarily halt or pause trading during extreme market price drops to curb panic selling, allow information dissemination, and restore market stability.

The difference between the current spot price of an underlying asset and the futures price of the corresponding contract.

The risk that the spread between the spot price of a hedged asset and the futures contract price fluctuates unpredictably during the hedge.

The simultaneous purchase and sale of stock index futures and the underlying basket of stocks to exploit deviations from fair theoretical value.

Define: Prepayment Risk

The risk that borrowers accelerate principal payoffs on fixed-rate loans when interest rates fall, shortening asset duration.

Exchange-mandated trading pauses triggered when a market index drops by specified percentages to prevent panic liquidations.
